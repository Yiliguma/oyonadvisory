Japan's Green Tea Market: Growth Meets a Supply Constraint

September 29, 2026

A mature bottled tea market and fast-growing export demand now depend on a shrinking base of tea fields. The next opportunity lies in making the supply chain more adaptable.

By Yili · Research and drafting support by ChatGPT

Share on LinkedIn

Japanese green tea is often described as a single growth story. In practice, it contains several businesses moving at different speeds. Conventional loose-leaf consumption has faced long-term pressure. Bottled tea is already a large and mature domestic category. Matcha and other powdered teas, meanwhile, are finding new customers abroad. Each business draws on the same agricultural base, but asks different things of growers and processors.

The supply base is narrowing. Japan had approximately 33,000 hectares of tea cultivation in 2025, down 4.8% year on year, while crude tea output was roughly 75,000 tonnes. Green tea exports reached about ¥72.1 billion, almost double the previous year's value. Export value, harvested tea tonnage, and beverage sales measure different stages of the chain; together, they show demand changing faster than the underlying production system.[1][2]

A large beverage market with limited volume growth

Japan produced 5.61 million kilolitres of tea-style drinks in 2025, 0.6% less than in 2024. Their producer sales value rose 2.3% to approximately ¥950 billion. Green tea drinks, a subset of that category, accounted for about 3.05 million kilolitres and ¥525 billion, with both volume and value slightly lower than a year earlier. Higher value in the wider tea category should therefore not be mistaken for strong volume growth in bottled green tea.[3]

Ito En demonstrates the different economics within this market. Its Oi Ocha brand needs consistent tea at beverage scale, while its matcha products and overseas activity require suitable leaves, milling, quality assurance, and market-specific products. The company says it handles about a quarter of Japan's crude tea production, giving it a substantial stake in the resilience of domestic growing regions.[4]

The scarce resource is suitable, reliable supply

Export growth does not automatically translate into more matcha. Tencha, the tea processed into matcha, requires shading before harvest and a processing route distinct from sencha. Converting a field or factory is consequently a decision about labour, equipment, yield, quality, and future demand. A higher spot price alone does not remove those constraints.

Automation could help at the field level. AutoCover is developing a drone-based system to deploy shading materials, an operation that can be labour-intensive. Its announced tea-field demonstration was planned for autumn 2026. The relevant test is whether it works reliably across actual field shapes and weather conditions while preserving the leaf quality buyers require; its impact is not yet established at commercial scale.[5]

At the factory level, Ito En has announced a hybrid production approach intended to make sencha and tencha in the same factory according to demand. This suggests flexibility is already becoming an operating priority. The public announcement does not disclose enough to establish conversion time, throughput, yield, or economics, so those remain useful questions for equipment and process innovation.[6]

Digital tools must solve a specific field problem

When a buyer works with many farms, it must know where tea was grown, what was applied to it, and whether it meets the requirements of a destination market. Ito En has partnered with WaterCell around the Agrinote farm management platform and jointly developed a system to check the suitability of agricultural chemicals for export-bound tea. That is a concrete use of agricultural data in procurement, rather than digitization for its own sake.[7]

Other tools may add value where they fill a remaining gap. Sagri, for example, uses satellite and soil data to visualize field conditions and guide management decisions. A tea-specific application would need to demonstrate accurate predictions across regions and varieties, fit into growers' workflows, and improve a measurable outcome such as fertilizer use, yield, or procurement reliability.[8]

Cost pressure continues after the leaves leave the farm

In bottled tea, packaging, filling, transport, and factory utilization matter alongside tea prices. Ito En and Toyo Seikan developed a filling system associated with lighter PET bottles. Ito En has also worked with Kirin Beverage to produce Oi Ocha in Hokkaido rather than transporting all of that region's supply from a more distant plant. These examples point to practical gains through production design and logistics, including areas where competitors can cooperate.[9][10]

That sets a high bar for a new packaging technology: it must work with established lines and improve total delivered cost or environmental performance, not merely demonstrate an attractive material in isolation.

Spent tea can become an input

Tea extraction creates another physical challenge. Ito En reported approximately 59,000 tonnes of spent tea from its beverage operations in fiscal 2024. Because the residue is wet and bulky, its best use depends heavily on where it is generated and what facilities are nearby.[11]

Ito En has explored several routes: tea-residue-containing sheets and industrial materials; fertilizer returned to contracted tea fields; biochar made using tea residue and coffee grounds; and ink incorporating spent tea for packaging applications. These projects show that tea residue has more than one possible destination. They do not establish that every route will be economical at every factory.[11][12][13]

TOWING's work on microbe-enhanced biochar from unused biomass illustrates a related technology area, although the cited material does not establish an Ito En partnership. For any tea-residue project, the decisive measure is value after collection, drying or conversion, transport, and field performance are accounted for.[14]

What the broader tea industry is learning

Global tea businesses face different crops and supply structures, but their responses offer a useful comparison. Lipton Teas and Infusions emphasizes agronomy, climate action, and supplier monitoring. Tata Consumer Products reports work on grower training, pest management, and traceability. Twinings and Bettys & Taylors describe longer-term programmes with growers and suppliers. These are investments in dependable origin supply as much as sustainability initiatives.[15][16][17][18]

Ito En's research adds a consumer-facing dimension. Its collaboration with ACCELStars studies the relationship between tea-drinking habits, sleep, and mental health. The work signals interest in evidence-led product differentiation, but an announced study should not be read as proof of a broad health effect.[19]

Where to look next

The clearest near-term technology opportunities are labour-saving cultivation for shaded tea, flexible tencha and sencha processing, and field systems that connect quality and export compliance to procurement decisions. On the beverage side, the opportunities are improvements to manufacturing and packaging that can be adopted at scale. Tea-residue products are promising where a local supply of wet material and a nearby use produce a credible economic loop.

The common test is straightforward: does a technology increase reliable, saleable tea supply or reduce the full cost of delivering it? Japan's export surge makes the question urgent. The contracting cultivation base makes a convincing answer valuable well beyond matcha.
